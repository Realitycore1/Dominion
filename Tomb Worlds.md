Tomb Worlds

Summary

A Tomb World is a planet, moon, habitat, megastructure, or artificial world whose dominant identity is shaped by mass death, civilizational extinction, preserved remains, failed settlement, ritual burial, battlefield aftermath, or the long-term habitation of ruins.

In simple terms:

A Tomb World is a world where death became the landscape.

A Tomb World is not merely abandoned.

It is defined by the scale and persistence of death.

It may contain:

* dead cities
* buried civilizations
* planetary gravefields
* corpse-vaults
* memorial continents
* extinction sites
* post-bombardment ruins
* irradiated settlements
* mass burial infrastructure
* preserved battlefields
* dead ecumenopolis layers
* sealed plague cities
* necropolises
* ghost infrastructure
* automated systems still serving populations that no longer exist

Some Tomb Worlds are sacred.

Some are cursed.

Some are archaeological treasures.

Some are military exclusion zones.

Some are traps.

⸻

Defining Traits

Tomb Worlds commonly contain:

* ruined cities
* mass graves
* necropolis districts
* dead orbital rings
* collapsed habitats
* memorial fields
* sealed bunkers
* battlefield wreckage
* preserved corpses or skeletonized remains
* abandoned infrastructure
* automated defense systems
* radiation zones
* plague markers
* vitrified ground
* impact scars
* silent transit systems
* abandoned data networks
* funerary megastructures
* corpse-processing ruins
* extinction monuments
* cultural remains
* damaged archives
* scavenger settlements
* forbidden excavation zones

A Tomb World’s defining feature is that the dead remain socially, physically, politically, or metaphysically present.

They are not gone.

They shape what the world is.

⸻

Primary Function

Tomb Worlds may serve as:

* memorial worlds
* archaeological worlds
* forbidden ruins
* mass burial sites
* war-crime evidence sites
* disaster exclusion zones
* necropolis worlds
* post-collapse salvage worlds
* sacred mourning worlds
* extinction record sites
* plague-sealed worlds
* military dead zones
* haunted cultural memory sites
* corpse-preservation worlds
* warning monuments
* historical investigation zones

In some civilizations, Tomb Worlds are protected so the dead are not disturbed.

In others, they are stripped for salvage.

That contrast can tell the reader everything about a faction.

⸻

Governmental Role

Tomb Worlds require special governance because they combine history, law, grief, danger, and ownership.

They may be administered as:

* protected memorial jurisdictions
* archaeological preserves
* restricted salvage zones
* war-crime investigation sites
* sacred cemetery worlds
* forbidden military zones
* quarantine-adjacent ruins
* cultural heritage worlds
* noble burial domains
* extinct-civilization preserves
* evidence-protection worlds
* disaster recovery sites

Governments must answer hard questions:

* Who owns the dead?
* Who may excavate?
* Who may salvage?
* Who may settle?
* Who may memorialize?
* Who decides whether ruins are sacred, useful, or dangerous?
* What happens if the dead belong to an extinct civilization?
* What happens if the dead are your enemies?
* What happens if the ruins contain technology needed by the living?

A Tomb World is never just a ruin.

It is a legal and moral argument.

⸻

Economic Role

Tomb Worlds usually have unstable or restricted economies.

They may export:

* archaeological data
* recovered artifacts
* salvage metals
* ancient technology
* historical records
* funerary goods
* genetic samples
* war-crime evidence
* cultural reconstructions
* preserved biological material
* relics
* antique machinery
* battlefield scrap
* memorial tourism
* forensic expertise

They import:

* archaeologists
* forensic teams
* security forces
* preservation equipment
* hazard suits
* radiation shielding
* decontamination gear
* legal observers
* religious custodians
* salvage crews
* drones
* medical supplies
* containment systems

The darker economy is obvious:

* corpse theft
* illegal relic trade
* grave robbery
* memory extraction
* black-market artifacts
* war trophy trafficking
* genetic piracy
* scavenger fiefdoms
* necro-industrial exploitation

Tomb Worlds become morally ugly when the dead are converted into resources without consent, memory, or law.

Social Character

Tomb World societies are shaped by grief, caution, superstition, opportunism, or reverence.

Common traits:

* quiet speech norms
* strong taboo systems
* scavenger codes
* funerary rituals
* respect for ruins
* fear of sealed zones
* memorial calendars
* forensic literacy
* distrust of outsiders
* fatalistic humor
* ancestor cults
* salvage guilds
* religious custodians
* historical obsession
* trauma inheritance
* ghost stories
* contamination anxiety

People who live on Tomb Worlds may become emotionally strange to outsiders.

They grow up with death as geography.

A strong cultural phrase:

The dead are not past here.

That should be core to this class.

Attacking a Tomb World may produce little tactical gain but enormous moral consequence.

⸻

Common Failure Modes

Desecration Economy

The dead are stripped for artifacts, bone, metal, memory, genetics, or historical value.

Salvage War

Competing factions fight over ruins, relics, archives, or old weapons.

Memorial Capture

A government weaponizes grief to justify policy, revenge, nationalism, or holy war.

Historical Suppression

Authorities restrict excavation because the truth of the world’s death threatens legitimacy.

Hazard Awakening

Ancient systems, plagues, AI, relics, Aether scars, or Domain residue reactivate.

Grave Plague

Preserved biological hazards emerge from corpses, crypts, or sealed hospitals.

Settler Irreverence

New colonists treat the world as free real estate and trigger cultural or supernatural-like backlash.

Custodian Tyranny

Memorial orders control access so rigidly that history becomes frozen and politically controlled.

Ghost Infrastructure

Automated systems continue operating according to dead priorities.

Mourning Stagnation

The world becomes unable to move beyond death; civilization freezes around grief.

⸻

Variants

War Tomb World

World defined by planetary war, invasion, bombardment, or battlefield ruins.

Plague Tomb World

Civilization died from disease, contamination, or failed quarantine.

Necropolis World

Purpose-built burial world, cemetery planet, or funerary megastructure.

Ruined Ecumenopolis

A dead city-world, often layered with abandoned urban strata.

Memorial Tomb World

Protected world dedicated to remembrance, pilgrimage, and mourning.

Salvage Tomb World

Ruins exploited for resources, artifacts, and old technology.

Forbidden Tomb World

Excavation banned due to danger, taboo, classified history, or political instability.

Royal Tomb World

Burial world of dynasties, emperors, saints, commanders, or noble bloodlines.

Xeno-Tomb World

Dead alien civilization preserved in ruins, fossils, archives, or megastructures.

Aether-Scar Tomb World

Mass death left metaphysical residue, Aether entity activity, memory echoes, or reality scars.
