The manhunt for Osama bin Laden was a 10-year effort by the United States and its allies to capture or kill the notorious Al-Qaeda leader, who was responsible for the September 11 attacks of 2001. The hunt began shortly after the attacks, when President George W. Bush vowed to "bring those responsible to justice." For the next decade, U.S. intelligence agencies and military forces tracked down leads on bin Laden's whereabouts, while also pursuing other high-value targets in Al-Qaeda's leadership structure. In 2011, after a decade of searching, U.S. special forces finally located bin Laden in Pakistan and launched a raid to capture or kill him. The raid was successful, and bin Laden was killed in the ensuing firefight. The capture of bin Laden was hailed as a major victory by the U.S. government and its allies, as it marked the end of a decade-long hunt and demonstrated the country's ability to carry out targeted military operations against high-value targets. However, the mission also raised questions about the legality of extrajudicial killings and the role of intelligence agencies in covert operations. Today, bin Laden's death is seen as a significant moment in the War on Terror, and his legacy continues to be debated and contested by scholars, politicians, and the public.
