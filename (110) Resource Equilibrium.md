# (110) Resource Equilibrium

## Manuscript Source

- Primary source: [[Manuscript]] Draft 2, Part III, Chapters 24-25.
- Manuscript date range: Years 101-122 After Landfall.

## What Happened

The first convoy departures forced New Aurora to confront resource equilibrium across distance. Leaving was not rebellion at first. It was a pressure release: land, shelter, geothermal access, food systems, education, family incentives, and route autonomy could not all remain centered on one city.

Settlements learned to balance dependence and self-rule through route ledgers, food-sharing contracts, medical delivery, weather scheduling, and train access.

## What Resulted

- Resource distribution became political.
- Convoys carried not just people but legal habits, welfare expectations, architecture, songs, seed stock, and local grievances.
- Overpopulation pressure and biologically long-lived bodies made family policy a permanent state concern.
- Clones, genetic hybrids, and engineered labor began appearing in ethical debates around workforce capacity and welfare burden.

## Consequences

Resource equilibrium became the nonviolent ancestor of later power blocs. Each settlement asked the same question differently: what does New Aurora owe us, and what do we owe New Aurora?
